# English

- Keep the established US or UK spelling, punctuation, and date conventions.
  For a new draft, use the audience's convention when known and stay consistent.
- Put modifiers beside what they modify. Repair dangling participles and unclear
  pronouns: “After reviewing the logs, we found the error,” rather than making
  the error do the reviewing. Do not invent “we” if the actor is unknown.
- Check subject–verb agreement, articles, countability, tense, possessives, and
  parallel constructions. A long noun phrase between subject and verb can hide
  an agreement error. Use the tense required by the event and genre.
- Join independent clauses with a period, semicolon, or suitable conjunction;
  do not repair a comma splice by introducing a causal link the source lacks.
- Prefer the normal collocation: “discuss the issue,” “explain it to the team,”
  “evidence for the claim.” Repair translation-shaped syntax as a whole rather
  than replacing isolated words.
- Use plain verbs where the meaning permits: “decide” for “make a decision,”
  “use” for “make use of.” Keep technical nouns that identify a concept under
  discussion. Passive constructions remain useful when the actor is irrelevant.
- Separate politeness from uncertainty. “Could you send the file by Friday?”
  is a request, not a weak factual claim. “The treatment may help” conveys
  uncertainty and must not become “The treatment helps.”
- Check false friends when translating from Italian: “eventualmente” may mean
  “if needed,” “attualmente” means “currently,” and “sensibile” may mean
  “sensitive.” Select by context; avoid a fixed substitution table.
- Preserve the author's contractions, humor, and level of directness where they
  fit the audience. Do not add a casual voice to formal correspondence.

For numerical prose, keep units, denominators, and comparison bases explicit.
A fall from 20% to 15% is five percentage points, or a 25% relative reduction;
those expressions cannot be exchanged without preserving the base.
