# Prose craft

## Compose before polishing

Write a private one-sentence brief: who is reading, why they are reading, and
what they should take away. Select facts that serve that brief. Put the answer,
request, or finding where the reader expects it; a narrative may need context
first. Draft the substance before tuning individual words.

Build paragraphs around a question, explanation, scene, or action. Let the
reader follow an entity or idea across sentences. Repeat the exact noun when
a pronoun would be ambiguous; do not cycle through synonyms for one technical
concept. Put the sentence's emphasis where it serves the argument, often at
the end. Move a qualification only if its scope remains clear.

For instructions, state the action, required input, and outcome together. For
an email, make its purpose and any request easy to find. For an explanation,
connect a mechanism to an observable consequence. For public copy, describe
what someone can do and what supports the claim. Invented benefits are not
an acceptable substitute for missing facts.

## Repair the cause of weak prose

| Problem | Useful intervention | Preserve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Setup delays the point | Begin at the actual claim or request | Context needed to interpret it |
| Action hidden in nouns | Give the actor a verb | Established concepts such as “classification” |
| Abstract praise | State the supported mechanism or outcome | The author's intended enthusiasm |
| Stock contrast or triad | Explain the real distinction; keep only useful items | Deliberate parallelism |
| Every paragraph repeats the same shape | Reorganize around the development of ideas | Logical dependencies |
| Qualifications bury the verb | Separate background from the main clause | The qualifier's scope and force |
| Bureaucratic or translated phrasing | Recast the sentence in the target language | Meaning and register |
| Closing sentence repeats the paragraph | Remove it or add the next implication | Necessary recaps in long documents |

Do not make brevity the objective when it costs intelligibility. A defined term,
a second example, or an explicit logical connection can save the reader effort.
Fragments can suit labels and conversational prose; complete sentences suit
most explanations. The genre decides.

## Fidelity examples

Given “The trial may reduce delays for some teams; costs were not measured,”
write “The trial may shorten delays for some teams. We did not measure costs”
only if “we” names the original investigators. Otherwise retain “Costs were not
measured.” “The trial cuts delays and costs” invents two conclusions.

Given “We are delighted to announce a transformative update that empowers you
to export invoices as CSV,” write “You can now export invoices as CSV.” Keep a
celebratory tone if the brief calls for one, but ground the benefit in the actual
feature. Do not invent time savings.

Given “Alla luce delle problematiche emerse, si procederà a una revisione delle
scadenze,” write “Visti i problemi emersi, le scadenze saranno riviste.” If the
source identifies the team responsible, name it; otherwise do not supply one.

Read the result for sense and cadence. If an edit is merely different, keep the
clearer existing wording. Recheck any sentence whose certainty or emphasis
changed during polishing.
