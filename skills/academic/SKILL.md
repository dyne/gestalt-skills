# Academic writing

Make the research question, contribution, and evidential limits inspectable.
Adapt the document to its discipline and article type; do not impose an
experimental-paper template on a proof, essay, review, or qualitative study.

## Workflow

1. Establish the requested section or document, discipline, audience, article
   type, length, and citation convention. Inspect the supplied manuscript,
   evidence, sources, and applicable author instructions. For a local edit,
   read the surrounding argument without reopening unrelated sections.
2. Map the argument privately: question → claim → evidence → warrant → limit.
   Separate reported findings, other authors' positions, and the writer's
   interpretation. Identify the most consequential unsupported link before
   drafting. A gap in evidence requires a narrower claim or a visible question,
   not a fluent invented bridge.
3. Organize around the contribution and its dependencies. Give each section a
   research function and each paragraph an argumentative task. Use familiar
   disciplinary concepts to introduce unfamiliar ones; keep the central terms
   stable across the argument. Draft the abstract after the core claims settle.
4. Write only from available evidence. Preserve the distinction between an
   association and a causal estimate, a hypothesis and a finding, a proof and a
   conjecture, a reported observation and an independently reproduced result.
   Retain the population, setting, assumptions, time frame, units, denominators,
   uncertainty, and counterevidence that bound the claim. Do not invent sample
   sizes, statistics, quotations, references, methods, ethics approvals, or data.
5. Integrate citations where the supported claim is made. Use narrative citation
   when a scholar's position matters and parenthetical citation when the finding
   matters. Verify the source's identity and its actual support from inspected
   material. A bibliography match alone does not verify a claim. If a source is
   unavailable, attribute the limited evidence or mark the citation for checking;
   do not imply full-text verification.
6. Revise for argument before sentence polish: test section order, missing
   warrants, alternative explanations, contradictory findings, and claims that
   outrun the design. Keep a reverse outline for substantial revisions: record
   each paragraph's task and remove or relocate passages that do not advance it.
   Explain how the reader should interpret the evidence rather than merely
   repeating a table or citing a string of studies.
7. Check the final text against the supplied results and sources. Preserve
   established terminology and any meaningful qualification. Follow the
   requested deliverable; if unresolved evidence prevents a final claim, provide
   the usable draft with the precise gap identified.

Use the entrypoint alone for a straightforward local edit. No external skill,
citation-verifier script, or database is required. Use available research tools
when source acquisition is part of the task; never claim a check you could not
perform.

## Delivery

Return the requested scholarly text or file edits. For a substantive revision,
briefly identify changes to the argument and any author decisions still needed.
For an assessment, locate each important claim, show the supporting evidence
or gap, and suggest the smallest defensible repair. Separate source checking,
methodological appraisal, and reproduction in any validation report.
