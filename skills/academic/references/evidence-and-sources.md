# Evidence and sources

## Synthesize around questions

Group studies by the question or claim they address, then compare methods,
populations, measures, assumptions, and results. Explain a disagreement before
calling it a contradiction: the studies may concern different outcomes or
settings. Separate what a review reports from the primary work you inspected.
An abstract-only reading supports a narrower account than a full-paper reading.

For a substantial synthesis, keep a compact working table with source/version,
claim, evidence location, relevant design, and limit. This is a drafting aid,
not a mandatory extra deliverable. Use one source to support several claims
only when the inspected material supports each one. A cluster of citations is
not evidence of consensus without a comparison of what they found.

For paper assessment, identify the inspected version and coverage, follow the
question through methods and results, and locate supporting passages, figures,
or tables. Align protocols and metrics before comparing numbers across papers.
Do not call an apparent algebraic consistency check an independent proof audit.

## Verify what a citation does

1. Establish identity: authors, title, year, version, and identifier where known.
2. Read the passage or result supporting the claim and note its location.
3. Check population, scope, assumptions, qualifications, and whether the source
   is making the claim itself or citing someone else.
4. Match the citation to the statement it actually supports. Preserve existing
   citation keys; change bibliographic facts only with evidence.

Quote when wording itself matters, with the required locator. Otherwise
paraphrase the underlying idea, not a sequence of synonym substitutions.
Retain attribution and distinguish the author's voice from the source's.
Never invent a DOI, page, quotation, or plausible-looking reference. An unresolved
`[source needed: ...]` marker belongs in an explicitly incomplete draft, not
in a document presented as ready for submission.

## Report empirical claims faithfully

- Keep effect size, uncertainty interval, sample or denominator, metric, and
  comparison aligned with the supplied analysis. Do not reconstruct missing
  precision or significance from a rounded value or graph.
- Distinguish statistical significance from practical importance. A result
  with an interval crossing the null does not establish equivalence. An absent
  significance test does not authorize the word “significant.”
- Separate percentage points from relative percent changes, raw from adjusted
  estimates, within-group changes from between-group effects, and training
  performance from held-out performance.
- Preserve causal limits. An observational association requires an identified
  causal argument before it becomes a causal claim; randomization also has a
  defined intervention, estimand, and population.
- Account for selection, missingness, measurement, multiplicity, and alternative
  explanations when they materially limit interpretation. State what the data
  do not resolve; avoid both exaggerated certainty and indiscriminate doubt.

For example, if the supplied result is a cross-sectional association between
sleep duration and errors, the discussion can describe that association and
the limits of the design. It cannot claim that increasing sleep prevents errors
or name a biological mechanism without additional evidence.
