# Research structures

Select only the structures useful for the article type and target requirements.
Follow supplied venue instructions instead of generic section expectations.

| Component | Research function | Failure to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Title | Identify the object and contribution at the supported scope | A causal or universal claim the paper cannot establish |
| Abstract | State the question, approach, main finding, and bounded contribution | Background that crowds out the actual result |
| Introduction | Establish the problem, consequential gap, and paper's answer | Declaring novelty without checking the relevant literature |
| Related work | Position the contribution through relevant agreements and differences | A source-by-source catalogue with no synthesis |
| Methods | Explain how evidence was generated and analyzed | Filling missing reproducibility details from convention |
| Results | Present the findings needed to answer the question | Interpreting nonsignificance as proof of no effect |
| Discussion | Interpret findings, compare explanations, define limits | Claiming a mechanism the design did not test |
| Conclusion | Give the supported answer and its consequence | Expanding scope or introducing new evidence |

For an empirical abstract, include a sample, outcome, and uncertainty only when
supplied. For a theoretical abstract, identify the objects, result, assumptions,
and advance. For humanities work, center the interpretive thesis, material, and
stakes. Structured-abstract headings are required only when the target says so.

In a methods section, describe the actual design, selection, materials,
procedures, analysis, and treatment of missing data as relevant. Ask for absent
details needed to interpret the result; a marked draft gap is preferable to
an invented procedure. Record prospective versus post hoc analyses accurately.

Use a discussion to distinguish what the finding supports from plausible
explanations. Name limitations in terms of their effect on interpretation or
generalization; do not append an interchangeable list of small-sample warnings.
Recommend future work that resolves a specific uncertainty.

## Discipline and genre

- In experimental and observational work, make design and outcome distinctions
  visible. Follow the field's reporting convention; first person or passive
  voice is a venue and rhetorical choice, not a disciplinary law.
- In qualitative work, identify the sampling logic, analytic approach, evidence
  excerpts, and relevant reflexivity. Do not force quantitative claims or
  saturation language onto work that does not support them.
- In humanities work, connect the interpretive claim to the passage, object, or
  historical evidence it depends on, then address a consequential alternative
  reading. Preserve ambiguity that the argument examines.
- In theoretical work, organize exposition around the main result and its
  dependencies. Distinguish an established input, the new argument, and an
  open question. Do not replace a proof obligation with rhetorical confidence.
- In interdisciplinary work, explain terms whose meanings differ across the
  participating fields before building an argument with them.

## Revision and reviewer responses

For a whole-document revision, settle the contribution, reverse-outline the
body, repair missing evidence or warrants, then reconcile the title, abstract,
and conclusion with the revised argument. Retain the author's strongest
supported thesis; “clarity” does not authorize a different position.

For each reviewer point, state the response, change or reasoned disagreement,
and exact manuscript location. Quote comments accurately. Describe a new
analysis as completed only if it was actually performed; distinguish proposed
work from a reported result. Do not invent line numbers or concede claims
solely to make the response sound agreeable.
